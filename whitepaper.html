talkmy™

White Paper

The AI for everyone. Settled 2026-11-16, updated 2026-11-18.

Talkmy™ is a voice-first AI app for seniors. One screen, one bright button, a chat thread. Press and hold, speak in any language, get an answer read aloud in that language. No sign-in, no account, no language picker, no typing required. The north star: a universal translator communicator: press button and get answers.

A note from the founder

The founder, Tony, states where Talkmy came from in two statements:

“This app was made with love, the founders love for his parents. Every decision was carefully thought out, and every one survived real testing.”

“I carved it in the only shape I knew, strong intelligent immigrant parents that were gated from the best of what their environment had to offer.”

1. The problem

Seniors are locked out of AI by the door, not by the room

The AI itself is not the barrier. A senior who can hold a conversation can use a conversational AI. The barrier is everything around it: an account to create, a password to keep, a prompt box that assumes typing, a language setting buried in a menu. The mainstream tools have no senior mode. The teaching lane, free classes and paid courses, proves the demand is real, then ends every lesson by handing the senior a stock account at the mainstream tool, sign-up wall and prompt box intact.

The adult child is the unpaid front line

Every family has one person who takes the questions. Is this junk mail? Is this real? What does this letter say? The questions are small. They arrive anyway, all day, at the worst times, at work, at dinner, at the end of a long day when the person answering is hungry and tired. The founder's statement of the problem is personal, because it is his:

“It's mentally exhausting to the point that sometimes I dread going over there and eating with them because they'll ask me questions I'm too overwhelmed to deal with. I just want to eat.”

The friction is not a lack of love. It is a routing problem. Every small question in two households routes to one person, because there is nowhere else for it to go.

The bother tax, and the scam door

The parent pays a tax the child never sees: hesitation. A parent who does not want to bother a busy son simply does not ask. The letter sits unopened, and an unanswered question is exactly how a scam gets in. The fake bank letter, the gift-card bail story, the urgent wire request all depend on the senior having no one to ask in the moment. Asking has to cost nothing, or people will not ask, and the door stays open.

2. The insight

The customer is the child. The user is the parent.

Talkmy has two people in it. The parent uses it. The adult child buys it, installs it, and gives the one sentence of briefing. Payer, installer, and briefer are the same person. The promise to them:

“Talk about how your parents are doing, not what they need done.”

Talkmy absorbs the chores so the calls home become conversation about their lives again. What still reaches the child is what deserves a child's brain, in the founder's success criterion, reducing my help to just judgement calls.

Solve for me, build for others

Talkmy was built to solve the founder's own nightly problem first. That is not a limitation; it is its redundancy. The worst case is bounded in advance: user number one is served every night, so the work cannot go to zero. It is the founder's root decision principle, don't make decisions until you can visualize the escapes, applied to building.

Nobody ships this shape

A landscape scan (2026-11-16) found the field split into three crowded lanes and one empty one: AI companions sold by the month (ElliQ, Sage, Tiina, Meela); simplified hardware (GrandPad, a locked tablet the family administers, inert without its plan); and teaching services that end at the stock ChatGPT login. The empty lane: no product found whose job is to be the front door to general AI for a senior: open the app, speak in plain words in your own language, get real general-purpose help, with no account ceremony, no prompt craft, no companion character, and no family dashboard watching. Custody in the field is inverted: conversations live on the maker's servers and die when payment stops. Talkmy inverts the doors back. 3. The product

The chain

  1. Open the app, you are in it. No landing page, no gate, nothing to click first.
  2. Glass hellos. Floating, quiet glass bubbles drift in the chat area, hello in twenty-three languages, in groups of about seven, dissolving and replaced. Nothing bright: brightness is a budget, and the whole budget is spent on the Talk button. Each bubble carries its instruction inside it, in its own language: the greeting, plus “Hold talk to speak.”
  3. Tap, or speak. Tap her language's bubble and every other hello disappears while the whole screen fades out and reappears in her language. Or simply hold the button and speak, the same fade happens into the language she spoke.
  4. The manual, on time. Once her language is known, the chat box quietly holds starter text in that language: what she can say, what she can do. “I'm giving them the manual only when I know they can understand it.”
  5. Hold, speak, hear the answer. The button dents under her finger; a big marigold circle with a microphone appears at the center of the screen, with a countdown showing the time she has left, up to sixty seconds. The answer comes back read aloud first, in her language, one thought at a time. A photo can ride along with the question: show it a pill bottle or a letter and ask what is this, without typing a word.

The redundancy stack

No single layer carries the teaching. Each assumes the previous one might fail: the installer's one sentence (“You just use it like we chat, like a person, and you can send photos. I installed it for you. It looks like this.”); the floating hellos; the tap, Apple's setup screens taught the world that the greeting screen is where you tap your language, and Talkmy honors that convention instead of fighting it; speech detection itself; and a teaching pulse on the Talk button that runs across as many launches as it takes, until she clicks and completes a talk successfully, demonstrated understanding, not a timer, then goes still forever. The deepest layer is not in the app at all: a motivated adult child who would gladly spend one kitchen-table hour to buy back years of small stuff.

The hellos, precisely

Twenty-three languages, in three groups, ordered by population weighted by likely users, with the main seven drawn from the region the phone already reports, no account, no asking.

A phone in California floats English, Spanish, Chinese, Vietnamese, Tagalog, and Korean first; a phone in Bavaria floats German, Turkish, Polish, Russian, and Arabic. Grouped, nobody is ever more than about two dissolves, roughly twenty-four seconds, from their language. On a tap or a first message, the crowd bows out and only her language remains.

The acceptance test

“If they can't understand what to do when they open my app, we've failed.”

The parity check that runs it: the founder installs Talkmy on his father's phone, gives the one sentence, then watches silently. His parents are the first beta testers.

4. The doctrines

No unexpecteds

“Unexpecteds are what causes a break.” For a senior mid-thought, a surprise is the thread snapping: where did that come from, what did I press, did I break it. Predictability is the accessibility feature: everything happens where it always happens.

All speech lives in the chat box

“Any speech that needs to come up, goes in the chat box.” Answers, prompts, the starter manual, deferral lines, all of it arrives as text in the thread. No pop-ups, no overlays.

Know, or defer

The scam guard is built in and deliberately not advertised, an advertised protection is a promise, and a promise creates a duty; unadvertised, it is a kindness. Its law: either it knows, or defers. Know: grounded in what was actually shown, naming only the concrete signs genuinely present (urgency, gift-card payment, a sender that does not match, a callback number), never inventing details. Ask: one small gentle request at a time. Defer to people: family, or the bank via the number on the back of her own card, never a number inside the suspicious message. The canonical line: “Let's check together.” Confidence never gets a vote.

Privacy by structure, not by policy

Talkmy only listens while the button is held. No ambient capture, no background recording. Her capsule, her language, her home place, how she likes answers given, is built locally on her phone, and the file itself never leaves it. A few facts from it ride along with each question as context, so the answer fits her; the file stays hers. Anyone can copy a button. The moat is the capsule, in the user's own custody. The full detail lives in the privacy policy.

The asking is never metered

No prompt caps on her screen, ever. Asking again costs nothing: every answer carries a round play button beside it, marked in her language with words from the say-it-again family, and tapping it reads the answer aloud again, as many times as she likes.

5. Architecture

  • The ears are a cloud function. When she holds Talk, the phone records a short clip, like a voice message. The clip travels to Talkmy's own cloud function, which has Gemini hear it, write down what she said, and answer. The clip is used for that one answer, is deleted from the phone when the hold finishes, and Talkmy keeps no copy. Typed words travel as text to the same brain, and a photo she sends rides along with her question. Only the last few exchanges ride as context; Talkmy stores no conversation on any server.
  • The brain: Gemini Flash. Voice clips reach it through the cloud function, on Vertex AI; typed questions reach it through Firebase AI Logic. Both routes are vouched for by App Check attestation, with no API key inside the app, and invisible anonymous authentication, so the no-sign-in doctrine holds. Distilled capsule facts shape each request as context; the capsule file itself never uploads.
  • The voice: spoken answers are usually made by Google Cloud Text-to-Speech, which receives only the answer's words, never her voice. If it cannot be reached, the phone's own built-in voice reads the answer instead.
  • There is no offline mode. An earlier design carried an on-device option; it was scrapped before the current version shipped, because a second, weaker brain made the app harder to trust and harder to use. Talkmy needs the internet to answer, and says so in one calm line, in her language, when there is none.
  • Weather, videos, and directions are hand-offs with a read-back. Weather answers come from a weather feed (Open-Meteo, with the US National Weather Service as backup) for the home place she told Talkmy in her own words. The app asks for no location permission and uses no GPS. YouTube and Maps open only after she agrees to a big card that reads the search or the destination back to her; Maps offers Apple Maps, and Google Maps too when it is installed and she chooses it.
  • Sharing is the phone's own share sheet. The Talkmy wordmark at the top left is a button. Tapping it, or the small teaching bubble under it, opens the share sheet with a short prewritten message in her language and the talkmy.ai link. What leaves the phone is only what she picks there.
  • The assistance contact. She may name one family member for assistance, picked by her in the phone's own contact picker; Talkmy never reads her address book. It stores only their name, role, phone number, and email, in the capsule on her phone, and only the name and role ever ride along as answer context, so a safety deferral can name the right person. Talkmy does not contact them on its own.
  • Bug reports travel by her own Mail. If she says something is wrong, Talkmy asks her to describe it in her own words, tells her plainly what a report contains, and presents her Mail app's compose screen, addressed to the maker. She taps Send. Nothing can be sent behind her back.
  • Platform order: iOS first, carved to finished; Android mirrors once. Never both in parallel.

6. Business model

This is the intended model, stated plainly: Talkmy is not on sale yet, and none of what follows is in the current build.

  • The core is never sold by the question. Asking is uncapped from the parent's side of the glass. She never sees a price tag on asking for help.
  • The children pay, and send it to their parents. Premium is bought by the adult child and gifted onto the parent's phone. The invoice and the gift wrap are the same box.
  • Premium carries the business: advanced features, and at the top a staffed service layer: live operators a senior can reach through the app, bought by family as peace of mind. The care features are never the advertised product.
  • Filtered advertisers, if advertising ever comes, ratified in advance: “Filtered advertisers. If we have to take less money because we're honest, then so be it. Won't let our seniors be prey.”
  • Pricing posture: honest production cost, not rent extraction. A great idea shared freely; a better evolution sold fairly.

7. Roadmap

  • Help hand-off: the gentle interview, the summary, the dedicated contact.
  • Fall detection, emergency contacts, suggested prompts: prompts only ever as soft invitations.
  • The child companion pipe: records and logs for the adult child, opt-in, for those who want always connected, for those with more needs. The records are the parent's; the child sees what she granted, and the grant is revocable.
  • Memory care: the far horizon, under a fixed boundary: “Digital care, not as the backbone, but as an additional tool.”

“Talkmy is the tool that allows for the load-bearing parts to be frictionless.”

8. State and honest limitations

State, 2026-11-18: name, icon, tagline, and domain locked; website live at talkmy.ai. The current app is Talkmy 2.0, build 9, running on the founder's test iPhones: hold-to-talk voice clips answered out loud by Gemini through Talkmy's cloud function, photo questions with tap-to-magnify answers, a play button beside each answer, spoken controls for the voice, the words, and the buttons, weather answers, YouTube and Maps cards, the share corner, and the on-device capsule. The app is not publicly released yet: there is no App Store or Google Play page for it, and this site links to none. The parents handover test is still the gate that matters.

Limitations, unsoftened:

  • The parents handover test has not been recorded yet. The founder's own hands have run the app end to end on real phones; his parents' first unassisted run is still ahead. Until it happens, every usability claim in this paper is a design claim, not a measured one.
  • Translations are best-effort. The words the app shows in its thirteen interface languages, and the starter texts in its twenty-three hello languages, are flagged for native review before any public launch.
  • The scam guard is a discipline, not a guarantee. Know-or-defer bounds its errors, but deferral depends on a human being reachable.
  • Bug reports depend on the phone's Mail being configured, which on many seniors' phones it is not. When it is not, the report stays as words in the conversation instead of being lost.
  • There is no offline mode, so no connection means no answers. Talkmy says so in one calm line rather than falling back to a weaker on-phone brain.
  • The app is iPhone only today. Android is a planned mirror of the finished iOS build, not a shipped app.
  • Distribution is a sentence, not a channel. The model assumes sons and daughters carry it phone to phone. That is a belief about how families work, held by a founder who lives it nightly, unproven at any scale beyond one family.
